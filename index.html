About

I'm a biomedical engineering graduate with a research practice that sits between hardware, signal processing, and applied machine learning. My undergraduate thesis built a facial EMG-controlled wheelchair system from the ground up — filtering raw muscle signal, extracting features, and eventually driving real hardware over Bluetooth. Since then I've stayed close to that same question in different forms: what can be read reliably from a noisy biological signal, and what can't be, no matter how sophisticated the model.

That instinct for honest evaluation carries into my current work, where I'm applying segmentation and quantitative imaging methods to pancreas MRI as part of a study on diabetes in the Indian population. I work primarily in Python, and I care about pipelines that are reproducible and results that hold up under scrutiny — including the null ones.

Facial EMG-Controlled Wheelchair

Two-phase system: Phase I captured and band-pass filtered (30–500 Hz) EMG signal on a BIOPAC MP45 for feature extraction; Phase II moved to threshold-based hardware control over HC-05 Bluetooth and an Arduino UNO.

Revisiting the EMG Dataset

A full Python pipeline — LOSO cross-validation, feature importance, SMOTE testing, a safety controller, and a PyBullet 3D simulation — built to re-examine the original dataset honestly rather than chase an inflated accuracy number.
